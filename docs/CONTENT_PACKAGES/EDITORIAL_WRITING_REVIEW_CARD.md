# Colin Michaels Editorial Writing Review Card

Use this one-page card before asking for a CMS import or publishing a draft. It does not replace source review, disclosure, accessibility, or package validation. It makes sure the finished post has a reason to exist beyond covering a topic.

## The decision to write

Do not begin with a broad subject such as "new AI tools" or "cool tech." Finish this sentence first:

> A reader will spend time with this because they want to know **[the concrete surprise, decision, or lived outcome]**, and I can give them **[the honest answer, useful distinction, or first-person lesson]**.

If that sentence is vague, narrow the idea or do not write it yet.

The current Colin Michaels lane remains:

- unusual gadget or internet discovery;
- practical technology, comparison, or useful fix; or
- tangible AI tied to a device, creator workflow, or visible outcome.

Personal recovery, music, projects, and FPV stories belong when Colin has a real moment, record, or lesson to share. They do not need to imitate a gadget post.

## The first 150 words

The opening should do three things, in this order:

1. Put the reader inside the surprising object, moment, problem, or choice.
2. Reveal the catch, tension, or reason it matters.
3. State Colin's honest relationship to the subject: tested, first-person, researched, or still unknown.

The disclosure must stay, but it should sound human. A researched post normally needs one direct opening sentence such as: "I have not used this myself, so I checked the current product pages and independent coverage instead." The detailed evidence, relationship, AI, and synthetic-media record belongs in the existing evidence card and source ledger unless a particular limitation changes the verdict.

Avoid opening with a stack of specifications, generic praise, or a long legal-style paragraph. Put the reader promise first; then earn trust with the boundary.

## The middle: proof, perspective, and payoff

Every substantial post needs all three:

- **Proof:** a supplied photo or video, a source-adjacent claim, a current price/order page, a real project artifact, a researched comparison, or clearly labeled uncertainty.
- **Perspective:** what surprised Colin, what he would do differently, the ordinary problem hidden beneath the novelty, or the practical consequence for a reader.
- **Payoff:** a usable answer: who it is for, who should skip it, which step to take, what to ask, or what lesson is worth carrying forward.

Use media to clarify the adjacent section, not to decorate the page. Two distinct inline images remain the completion minimum for full packages. A video, chart, table, poll, or checklist is useful only when it helps the reader reach the promised answer.

## The ending

End with a decision, not a recap. The reader should be able to repeat Colin's conclusion in one sentence.

If interaction fits, ask a question that requires a real choice:

- Good: "Would this solve a recurring problem in your house, or create a more expensive version of it?"
- Weak: "What do you think?"

Do not add a poll or comment prompt to sensitive medical content, and do not use a question as a substitute for a conclusion.

## Five-minute editorial check

Read the draft aloud once, then mark each line true or revise it.

- [ ] The title names the real object, moment, or outcome and does not oversell it.
- [ ] The first paragraph makes sense without search-engine context or a generic introduction.
- [ ] Colin's role is unmistakable: first-person experience, researched analysis, or a stated unknown.
- [ ] Facts, manufacturer claims, visible evidence, inference, and opinion are not blurred together.
- [ ] The article gives a reader a specific answer, checklist, distinction, or earned reflection.
- [ ] At least one sentence could only have been written in Colin's voice and perspective.
- [ ] Every visual, embed, table, chart, and interaction earns its place beside the nearby passage.
- [ ] The ending has a verdict before it asks the reader to act.

If two or more boxes are false, return to the outline rather than polishing sentences around a weak premise.

## What the current package drafts teach us

The HOVERAir AQUA, passenger-aircraft, Unitree R1, and Laundry Chair drafts are strongest when they name a concrete object, expose the catch quickly, state the no-hands-on boundary plainly, show source or video evidence early, and make a narrow verdict. Keep that pattern.

Their shared risk is front-loading a complete disclosure inventory before the reader reaches the useful distinction. Preserve every disclosure in the existing editorial metadata and evidence card, but use the lead to make the reader care first. One clear research boundary is usually enough in the opening; the later source and evidence sections can carry the full detail.

## Publish boundary

This card is a writing-quality check, not permission to publish. A post still requires current sources, appropriate rights, valid canonical CMS blocks, image and disclosure review, import validation, and the normal editorial approval process.
